The letter arrives a few weeks after the adjuster came out. It is one page, it uses the phrase "no covered damage was observed," and it does not explain much beyond that.

Most people read it and assume the conversation is over. It usually is not.

A denial is a position taken by one person who spent twenty minutes on your roof. It can be wrong, it can be incomplete, and it can be revisited. Not always — sometimes a denial is correct and we will tell you that. But it is worth understanding what the letter actually said before you accept it.

## Read the reason, not the verdict

Denials generally fall into a few categories, and they are not equally final.

**"Wear and tear" or "age-related deterioration."** The adjuster concluded your shingles failed because they are old, not because of a storm. This is the most common denial and the most commonly reversed one, because hail bruising on an older roof looks a lot like normal aging unless someone knows the difference and documents it properly.

**"Damage does not exceed your deductible."** They found damage but valued the repair below what you would pay out of pocket anyway. Sometimes accurate. Sometimes it means the scope missed things — the vents, the flashing, the gutters, the siding on the same elevation.

**"No covered damage observed."** They did not find storm damage. Worth asking: where did they actually look? Hail damage concentrates on certain slopes depending on the storm's direction. An adjuster who inspected the front of the house on a windy day may genuinely not have seen the south-facing slope where everything landed.

**"Reported outside the policy period."** Timing. This one is usually harder to move, and it is a good argument for reporting storms promptly even when you are not sure.

## Ask for the adjuster's report

You are entitled to the documentation behind the decision. Ask for the full report, including photographs and the scope of what was inspected.

This is the single most useful thing you can do, and most homeowners never do it. The report tells you what slopes they looked at, what test squares they marked, how many hits they counted, and what they measured. Very often it tells you exactly where the gap is.

## Understand what actually changes a decision

Calling to complain does not reverse a denial. What reverses a denial is new information presented in the format the carrier works in.

That usually looks like a documented re-inspection: a test square on each slope, photographs with a gauge and a date, a manufacturer's specification showing what that shingle is rated for, weather data for your address on the storm date, and a written scope of what a proper repair requires. Presented as a supplement to the existing claim, referencing the original claim number.

This is why the insurance side of roofing is a real skill and not a formality. A roofer who can install a roof beautifully and cannot document a claim will not get the claim paid.

## You can ask for a re-inspection

Most carriers will send someone back out if there is a specific reason to. "I disagree" is not a reason. "Your report shows three slopes inspected and my house has five, and here are dated photographs of hail impact on the two that were not examined" is a reason.

If the second inspection also denies it, Maryland homeowners have further options through the Maryland Insurance Administration, which handles complaints about claim handling. That is a real avenue and it costs nothing to use.

## Be honest with yourself about the roof

We would rather say this plainly: sometimes the denial is right.

If your roof is twenty-five years old and worn out, a hail storm did not cause that, and pushing a claim that has no merit wastes months and can affect how your carrier treats you going forward. In that situation the honest answer is that you need a roof and insurance is not going to buy it, and the conversation should be about what a replacement actually costs.

We tell people that. It is not the answer anyone wants, but it is better than a year of appeals that go nowhere.
