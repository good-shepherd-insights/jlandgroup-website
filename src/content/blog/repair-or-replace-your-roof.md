You get three estimates. One says patch it for six hundred dollars. One says you need a full replacement. One says replace it and while we are here you should do the siding too.

They looked at the same roof. That is frustrating, and it makes it hard to trust any of them.

Here is how to think about it yourself.

## Start with age, because it decides most of it

Architectural asphalt shingles in Maryland generally last around twenty to twenty-five years. Older three-tab shingles run shorter, often fifteen to twenty. Our climate is hard on roofs — hot humid summers, freeze-thaw cycling through the winter, and enough wind events to matter.

If your roof is past twenty years, repairs get harder to justify. Not because the repair will not work, but because you are paying for labor and a setup on a roof you will replace in three years anyway. And matching shingles to a twenty-year-old roof rarely looks right, because the existing ones have faded and the product line has usually changed.

If your roof is under twelve years old and something is wrong, it is almost always a repair. Roofs that young do not fail across the whole surface unless they were installed badly.

Between twelve and twenty is where it depends, and where the honest answer requires actually looking.

## Localized problem or system problem

This is the real question underneath repair-versus-replace.

**A localized problem** is one cause in one place. A branch came through. Flashing around the chimney pulled loose. A vent boot cracked — those rubber collars around plumbing vents dry out and split around the ten-year mark, and they are a very common source of leaks on otherwise fine roofs. Wind lifted shingles along one edge.

These are repairs. A good repair on a sound roof lasts.

**A system problem** shows up in more than one place at once. Granules filling your gutters across the whole run. Shingles curling or cupping on multiple slopes. Multiple leaks in unrelated parts of the house. Soft spots underfoot, which means the decking underneath is wet and rotting.

That is the roof telling you it is finished. Patching one leak on a roof like that just moves the problem.

## Things you can check from the ground

You do not need to climb up.

Look at your gutters after a rain. A little granule loss is normal. A gritty layer like coarse sand accumulating consistently means the shingles are shedding their protective surface.

Look at the roofline against the sky. It should be straight. Waves, dips, or sagging mean a structural or decking issue, and that is never a simple repair.

Look up at the slopes from across the street. Shingles should lie flat. Curled edges, cupping, or bald dark patches where granules are gone are all end-of-life signs.

Check your attic on a bright day. Daylight coming through the boards means gaps. Dark staining on the underside of the decking means water has been getting in, possibly for a long time.

## The question that separates honest estimates from bad ones

When a contractor recommends replacement, ask: **what specifically did you find, and can you show me?**

A real answer sounds like: "Your south slope has hail bruising, I marked a ten-by-ten test square and counted eleven hits, here are the photos, and the mat is fractured under the granule loss, which means it will leak within a year or two."

A bad answer sounds like: "It is in rough shape, they do not last forever, you are going to want to get ahead of this."

The second one may still be true. But someone who cannot show you what they found either did not look carefully or is not telling you everything.

## When replacement is genuinely the better buy

Sometimes replacement wins even when a repair would technically work:

- You are approaching the end of the roof's life and the repair is expensive
- There are multiple problem areas and each one is its own trip
- Insurance is covering the replacement — in which case the deductible is usually less than several out-of-pocket repairs
- You are selling within a year or two, and an old roof shows up in every inspection report and gets negotiated against you
